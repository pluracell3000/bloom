The central claim: a leading-edge semiconductor fab is not a capital asset you replicate but a *process organism* you cultivate, and this — not labor costs or permits — is the real reason overseas expansion is slow and expensive.

## Yield is the whole game

A modern fab runs wafers through well over a thousand process steps, any one of which can introduce a defect that kills a die. The fraction of chips that come out working — yield — is the difference between a money-printing machine and a $20 billion write-off. Early in a node's life, yields can sit below 50%; mature processes push above 90%. Because the fab's costs are almost entirely fixed, every point of yield is nearly pure margin. This is why the industry obsesses over it to a degree outsiders find strange.

## Knowledge lives in people, not manuals

The equipment inside a TSMC fab in Arizona is the same equipment as in Hsinchu — largely from the same vendors, ASML and Applied Materials and Tokyo Electron. What differs is everything around the machines: the recipe tweaks, the maintenance rhythms, the tribal knowledge of which chamber drifts in which direction after how many hours. This knowledge is accumulated by engineers running the process, watching it fail, and adjusting — and most of it is never written down because it changes faster than documentation can keep up.

## The metabolism argument

The deepest point is about *speed of correction*. Process drift is constant: tools age, chemistries vary between batches, ambient conditions shift. A fab's real capability is how fast its people notice an excursion, trace it to a root cause across a thousand candidate steps, and push a fix. TSMC's Taiwan fabs have engineers who will respond to a 2 a.m. alarm in person; the feedback loop runs in hours. A new site starts with a slower loop — new hires, unfamiliar failure signatures, decisions escalated across time zones — and a slower loop compounds into a persistent yield gap.

## So what

The lesson generalizes past chips: for any process industry where the product is made by iteration rather than assembly — pharma biologics, specialty chemicals, even aerospace composites — industrial policy that funds *buildings and machines* buys the cheap part. The expensive part is the decade of accumulated correction-speed, and the only known ways to transfer it are moving experienced people in bulk or accepting years of underperformance while the new site grows its own. Subsidy programs that measure success in facilities opened, rather than in yield curves converged, are measuring the wrong thing.
